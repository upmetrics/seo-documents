## 2. Thesis & Key Messages

### Thesis Statement

> Saltfern Parfums is asking for a $110,000 SBA 7(a) loan to launch a $75–$85 eau de parfum brand from Wrenfield, North Carolina. The plan rests on one idea: people buy perfume after they've worn it, so Saltfern spends its effort getting samples onto skin (discovery sets, travel sprays, and pop-up testers) and plans its cash around the production minimum that ties up $70,000 in stock before the first sale.

### Key Messages

1. **Sampling is the sales system.** Every channel starts with a sample: a $22 discovery set credited toward a 50 ml bottle (target: 25% convert within 60 days), testers and scent cards at about 40 pop-ups a year (~14% of people who smell the line buy), and a free 1 ml vial in every online order.
2. **The price gap is the position.** Designer 50 ml bottles sell for $120–$180 and niche houses for $200–$300. Saltfern sells niche-style compositions at $75–$85, with an 80% product margin at retail, which leaves room to sell wholesale at half price.
3. **Production minimums are the biggest risk, and the plan sizes for them.** The first run (600 bottles per scent) costs $70,000, 47% of startup capital, and covers about 12 months of 50 ml sales. Saltfern reorders only proven sellers at the filler's 500-bottle minimum, and uses discovery-set data to decide which scents to rerun.
4. **Each channel earns money differently.** After its share of marketing, online keeps 40.4% of revenue, wholesale 44.2%, and pop-ups 33.8%. Pop-ups keep the least, but they're where most customers smell Saltfern first.
5. **The numbers are honest about Year 1 and about cash.** Year 1 shows a $20,583 pre-tax loss because it absorbs $36,500 of one-time launch costs. Profit follows in Year 2 ($18,811) and Year 3 ($39,513). Cash bottoms out at $18,720 in October 2027, after the holiday reorder and before November–December, which bring in 32% of the year's sales.

## Why This Matters

- **Upmetrics has no fragrance plan.** The closest samples are candle making and soap making, both handmade, craft-scale businesses. Nothing covers a contract-manufactured brand selling online, at events, and wholesale.
- **Demand is growing at the price point Saltfern targets.** U.S. prestige fragrance sales rose 6% in the first half of 2026, eau de parfum grew at double digits, and mass fragrance grew 15% ([Circana, August 2026](https://www.circana.com/post/us-beauty-industry-posts-solid-first-half-growth-as-fragrance-and-skincare-lead-across-prestige-and)). Shoppers want stronger formulas, and many are price-sensitive: units in prestige were flat while prices rose 5%.
- **Published perfume plans don't add up.** The samples that rank for "perfume business plan" contradict their own funding and revenue figures, skip depreciation and loan repayment, or count vape shops and brow bars as competitors. None shows production minimums, channel margins, or a cash low point.
- **The real questions are operational.** Founders want to know how many bottles they must order, how much cash that ties up, whether wholesale at half price can work, and how samples turn into sales. This plan answers each with one set of numbers.
- **New rules raise the bar for 2026–2027 founders.** MoCRA (the 2022 federal cosmetics law) brought safety substantiation, adverse-event reporting, and labeling duties, and alcohol-based perfume ships by ground only unless the shipper has special authorization. A credible plan accounts for both.

**Q2:** Do I need FDA approval to sell perfume?
**A2:** Explain that perfume is regulated as a cosmetic, which doesn't need FDA approval before sale. Under MoCRA, the brand named on the label (the "responsible person") must have evidence each product is safe, report serious adverse events, and label products correctly; manufacturing facilities register with the FDA, and brands list their products unless a small-business exemption applies. Add that the rules are still being phased in (for example, fragrance allergen labeling), so founders should check the FDA's current guidance before launch. No link here (the FDA page is linked in the plan).
**Source:** FDA registration and listing page + editorial judgment
**Why not in main content:** The plan describes Saltfern's compliance steps; this answers the general question.

**Q3:** Should I make perfume myself or use a contract manufacturer?
**A3:** Say that making small batches yourself keeps minimums low and suits testing a scent at markets, but it limits volume and makes safety documentation, consistency, and wholesale harder. A contract filler brings consistent batches, batch records, and capacity, in exchange for minimums (often several hundred to a few thousand units per scent) and 6–12 week lead times. Many brands start by hand and move to a filler once one or two scents prove themselves.
**Source:** Reference 3 (filling minimums) + editorial judgment
**Why not in main content:** Saltfern has already chosen a contract filler; the plan doesn't weigh the alternative.

**Q4:** How do I adapt this sample plan for my own perfume brand?
**A4:** Tell readers to change five inputs first: the number of scents and formats, the filler's minimums and landed cost per unit, prices, channel mix (which sets the blended net revenue per unit), and units per month. Then rebuild the financial tables from those. Point out that the structure (product mix table, production and inventory chapter, channel economics, Year 1 monthly cash) stays the same.
**Source:** Editorial judgment
