## 2. Thesis & Key Messages

### Thesis Statement

> Haverton Urgent Care seeks $1,000,000 in SBA 7(a) financing to launch a physician-owned, single-location urgent care clinic in a fast-growing DFW suburb — on a conservative, credentialing-first plan that funds operations through the insurance-reimbursement lag and reaches profitability in Year 3.

### Key Messages

1. The Haverton market can support a new clinic because population growth and drive-time demand outpace existing urgent care capacity — shown with specific numbers, not assertions.
2. Daily patient volume, not optimism, drives the model: the clinic's operating break-even sits at roughly 24 visits/day, and the plan reaches that only in Year 2.
3. Payer credentialing must begin six to eight months before opening, because insurance contracts and provider enrollment can take 180+ days and determine when real revenue starts.
4. A physician-owner's early cash gap is a working-capital problem, not a profitability problem — visits ramp faster than collections, so the plan carries a reserve to cover it.
5. Clinical staffing is sized to demand, not to a fixed roster — the plan adds a second provider only when volume justifies the cost.

### Writing Tone

**For Sample Business Plan:**
- Write like a healthcare business consultant drafting a formal plan for Dr. Reed's lender.
- Third-person throughout — "Haverton Urgent Care will…" never "we" or "I."
- Every claim backed by a specific number, date, or named detail from Sections 1B and 6B.
- Use the pre-calculated tables from Section 6B exactly — do not invent new numbers.
- Use urgent-care vocabulary: visits per day, payer mix, credentialing, net revenue per visit, DSCR, occupational health, CLIA, throughput.
- Replace vague language with specifics: "22 visits per day in Year 1" not "modest early volume."

**General rules (all types):**
- Target reading level: Grade 8–9.
- Short paragraphs (3–4 sentences). Plain language, contractions, "you/your" only where addressing the reader directly (rare in a plan).
- No filler, no passive voice, no AI buzzwords. See Banned AI Terms in the Appendix.

### Section Priority Labels

- 🔴 **MUST HAVE** — essential to a lender-ready plan.
- 🟡 **SHOULD HAVE** — adds depth and credibility.
- 🟢 **NICE TO HAVE** — differentiation, can be trimmed if over length.

##### Executive Summary

>
> Hook: Open with the funding ask and the one-line business summary — "Haverton Urgent Care, PLLC, a physician-owned walk-in clinic, seeks $1,000,000 in SBA 7(a) financing to open in Haverton, Texas, in March 2027."
> Context: Who Dr. Reed is (board-certified EM physician, 12 years), what the clinic does (7-day walk-in care, on-site X-ray, labs, minor procedures, physicals, occupational health), and the opportunity (fast-growing suburb, limited walk-in capacity).
> Promise: What the lender will see — a conservative, credentialing-first plan that funds the reimbursement lag and reaches profitability in Year 3.

Place the internal link to [what lenders look for](https://upmetrics.co/blog/what-lenders-look-for-in-business-plan) after the funding-request sentence.

> 🎯 Our angle: Lead with the numbers a loan officer scans first — ask, owner equity, use of proceeds, break-even, Year 3 net income — so the summary reads like a credit memo, not a brochure.
> 🪝 Suggested hook: Open with the exact ask and the owner's ER credential in the first two sentences.

#### H2: Business Overview
> ~550 words, 5–6 paragraphs | 🔴 MUST HAVE
> 🎯 Our angle: Establish credibility through structure and ownership — a physician Medical Director satisfies both patients and payer/regulatory requirements.
> 🪝 Suggested hook: "Haverton Urgent Care, PLLC, is organized as a single-member professional limited liability company owned entirely by Dr. Allison Reed, MD."

Cover: the PLLC legal structure and why a physician practice uses a PLLC rather than a standard LLC (link [how to form an LLC](https://upmetrics.co/blog/how-to-form-an-llc) and, as a related example, [medical practice business plan](https://upmetrics.co/template/medical-practice-business-plan)); 100% ownership by Dr. Reed; the Medical Director role; mission (faster, lower-cost acute care for Haverton); the Haverton, TX location and ~3,000 sq ft leased space; business model (per-visit reimbursement + occupational-health contracts); and 1-year / 3-year goals (open March 2027, reach 30 visits/day by Year 2, operating profit by Year 3).

> 💡 ProTip: Note the single-member PLLC keeps ownership and medical liability with the licensed physician — a structure payers and state regulators expect.

#### H2: Market Analysis
> ~750 words, 7–8 paragraphs | 🔴 MUST HAVE
> 🎯 Our angle: Prove the market can support another clinic with drive-time demand and growth math — not a generic "urgent care is growing" claim.
> 🪝 Suggested hook: Open with the specific gap: "Haverton's population has grown roughly 4% a year, but the nearest urgent care is a 20-minute drive in moderate traffic."
> 🔗 Transition from previous: "With the business defined, the plan turns to the question every lender asks first: is there enough demand?"
> 🚫 Avoid: National statistics as a substitute for local analysis; citing competitors as sources.
> 📖 Reference: Experity's location-selection framework (demand, competition, visibility, payer saturation) — apply it to Haverton.

Cover: Haverton + adjacent exurb population (~62,000) and ~4%/yr growth; household demographics (young families, commuters, commercially insured); drive-time/visibility rationale for the chosen site; local demand drivers (ER overflow, pediatric minor injuries, employer physicals); payer landscape and mix (commercial majority, Medicare, modest self-pay); and the market-size estimate that supports ~36 visits/day by Year 3. Cite HIDA for the ~30 visits/day national average and ~200M annual visits, and Urgent Care Association for the 14,000+ centers context.

##### H3: Target Customer Profile
> 🔴 MUST HAVE — a dedicated subsection, not a sentence.
> Cover demographics (families 25–45, local employees, commuters), psychographics (convenience-driven, price-aware, avoid ER wait/cost), pain points (long ER waits, no same-day primary-care slots), buying behavior (walk-in, insurance-first, reviews-driven), and an estimated addressable visit volume for the primary trade area. Tie the profile back to the 22 → 36 visits/day ramp.

> 🖼️ Visual: Designed infographic (landscape ~900x450px): "Haverton trade-area snapshot" — population, growth %, insured %, nearest-competitor drive time, projected visits/day. Alt text: "Haverton urgent care trade-area demand snapshot."
> 💡 ProTip: Anchor the demand estimate to a capture rate of the trade-area population (e.g., visits/year ÷ population), so the lender can sanity-check it.

#### H2: Competitive Analysis
> ~550 words, 4–5 paragraphs + table | 🔴 MUST HAVE
> 🎯 Our angle: Position Haverton Urgent Care against the real alternatives patients weigh — not just other urgent cares, but the ER, retail clinics, and telehealth.
> 🪝 Suggested hook: "Patients in Haverton currently choose between a crowded hospital ER, a 20-minute drive to the nearest urgent care, or waiting days for a primary-care slot."
> 🔗 Transition from previous: "Demand alone doesn't justify a clinic — the plan now examines who already serves it."
> 🚫 Avoid: Naming or linking real competitors; inventing competitor financials.
> 📖 Reference: Experity's note on payer saturation and competitive visibility.

Cover: 2–3 fictional local/near competitors (another urgent care 20 minutes away, a hospital ER, a retail pharmacy clinic), compared on wait time, services, hours, X-ray availability, and cost. Then state Haverton Urgent Care's positioning: 7-day hours, on-site X-ray and labs (same-visit results), occupational-health contracts, and shorter drive time.

> 🖼️ Visual: Comparison table — columns: Provider | Hours | On-site X-ray | Typical wait | Services | Positioning note. Alt text: "Competitive comparison of Haverton-area acute-care options." Build this as an inline table.
> 💡 ProTip: On-site X-ray and same-visit lab results are a concrete differentiator against retail clinics — state it as a capability, not a slogan.

#### H2: Services & Clinical Capabilities
> ~700 words, 6–7 paragraphs | 🔴 MUST HAVE
> 🎯 Our angle: Describe each service line individually with what it is, how it's delivered, its price range, and its capacity — so the lender sees the revenue sources behind the per-visit model.
> 🪝 Suggested hook: "Haverton Urgent Care treats the acute, non-life-threatening conditions that account for most ER overuse."
> 🔗 Transition from previous: "Haverton Urgent Care's edge comes from the range of care it delivers under one roof."
> 🚫 Avoid: A bare bullet list of service names with no detail — each line needs delivery, pricing, and capacity.
> 📖 Reference: Experity's cost/equipment notes for the X-ray and lab lines.

Describe each individually: (1) Walk-in acute care (illness/injury) — the core visit; (2) On-site X-ray / radiology — same-visit imaging, capacity per day; (3) Laboratory & point-of-care diagnostics — rapid tests, basic labs; (4) Minor procedures — laceration repair, splinting, abscess drainage; (5) Physicals & immunizations — school/sports/DOT physicals, vaccines; (6) Occupational health — employer contracts for injury care, drug screens, pre-employment physicals. For each, state delivery format, typical price/reimbursement range, and daily capacity constraint (rooms, provider time, equipment).

> 🖼️ Visual: Inline table — "Service lines" (service | description | delivery | price range | daily capacity). Alt text: "Haverton Urgent Care service lines with pricing and capacity."
> 💡 ProTip: Occupational-health contracts smooth revenue because employers pay reliably and refer repeat volume — call this out as a cash-flow stabilizer.

#### H2: Marketing & Patient Acquisition Strategy
> ~550 words, 5–6 paragraphs | 🟡 SHOULD HAVE
> 🎯 Our angle: Frame payer credentialing as the true first step of go-to-market — being in-network is the acquisition channel that matters most.
> 🪝 Suggested hook: "The clinic's most important marketing decision happens before opening day: getting into insurance networks."
> 🔗 Transition from previous: "Offering these services means little if patients can't find the clinic — or can't use their insurance there."
> 🚫 Avoid: Generic digital-marketing filler; promising unrealistic ad ROI.

Cover: in-network status as the primary acquisition lever; local awareness (Google Business Profile, local SEO, signage, community events); employer outreach for occupational-health contracts; patient retention (reviews, text reminders, EMR-driven follow-up); and the Year 1 launch marketing budget ($35K one-time + $36K ongoing). Preview the credentialing chapter here.

> 🖼️ Visual: None required (keep lean). Optional simple funnel graphic: awareness → in-network → first visit → repeat.
> 💡 ProTip: A clinic that opens before it's in-network spends marketing dollars driving patients who then leave to use their insurance elsewhere — sequence credentialing first.

#### H2: Operations Plan
> ~800 words, 7–9 paragraphs | 🔴 MUST HAVE
> 🎯 Our angle: Show a clinic that runs on throughput and demand-matched staffing — the operational discipline that makes the financials achievable.
> 🪝 Suggested hook: "Haverton Urgent Care opens seven days a week, 8 a.m. to 8 p.m., with staffing that flexes to the hours patients actually walk in."
> 🔗 Transition from previous: "Winning patients is one thing; serving them efficiently is what protects the margin."
> 🚫 Avoid: Leaving hours, scheduling, the daily workflow, or the staffing schedule to the writer's discretion — all four are required below.
> 📖 Reference: Experity on throughput, scheduling, and demand-based staffing.

Cover all of the following as explicit subsections or named items:
- **Operating days and hours:** 7 days/week, 8 a.m.–8 p.m. (12-hour days), ~360 operating days/year; note holiday closures.
- **Patient flow & scheduling logic:** primarily walk-in with online check-in/queue; how visits are triaged; peak-hour handling (evenings, weekends).
- **A typical operating day:** morning open/stock/huddle → midday steady volume → evening peak → close/reconcile/clean. Walk the lender through it.
- **Staffing schedule tied to hours:** who works when (provider coverage across 12-hour days, MA/tech/front-desk shifts, how a second provider is added at peak once volume justifies it).
- **Facility & equipment:** ~3,000 sq ft, exam rooms, procedure room, X-ray suite, lab.

##### H3: Licensing & Regulatory Compliance
> 🔴 MUST HAVE
> Cover: state medical facility licensing, CLIA certification for the lab, radiology/X-ray equipment registration and tech licensing, the physician Medical Director requirement, OSHA/medical-waste compliance, and Texas corporate-practice-of-medicine considerations for a physician-owned entity.

> 🖼️ Visual: Process flowchart (landscape): "A day at Haverton Urgent Care" — open → triage → treat → imaging/labs → discharge/billing → close. Alt text: "Daily operational workflow for Haverton Urgent Care."
> 💡 ProTip: Staffing cost is the biggest controllable expense — matching provider hours to evening/weekend peaks protects margin better than cutting hours.

#### H2: Payer Credentialing & Revenue Cycle Strategy
> ~650 words, 5–6 paragraphs | 🔴 MUST HAVE
> 🎯 Our angle: This is the plan's information-gain centerpiece — explain *why* credentialing starts six to eight months before opening and *why* growing visits don't become cash right away.
> 🪝 Suggested hook: "A new clinic can be full of patients and still short on cash — because insurers pay 60 to 90 days after the visit, and only once the clinic is credentialed."
> 🔗 Transition from previous: "Smooth operations still depend on getting paid — and in urgent care, payment follows a timeline of its own."
> 🚫 Avoid: Treating credentialing as a one-line compliance task; implying revenue starts on opening day.
> 📖 Reference: JUCM credentialing article — the 90–180+ day timeline and the out-of-network collection problem.

Cover: the credentialing timeline (payer contracts 30–100+ days, provider credentialing 180+ days, activation 30–60 days) and why Dr. Reed begins applications to the top 5–7 payers in parallel before signing the lease; the reimbursement lag (visits in month 1, cash in month 3–4) and how it creates the Year 1 operating loss despite rising volume; the working-capital reserve ($398K) sized to bridge the gap; AR building to ~$117K by end of Year 1; and the revenue-cycle setup (billing/RCM, clean claims, denial management). Place internal links to [cash flow vs profit](https://upmetrics.co/blog/cash-flow-vs-profit) (in the visits-vs-cash paragraph) and [cash runway](https://upmetrics.co/blog/how-to-calculate-cash-runway) (where the reserve is justified).

> 🖼️ Visual: Timeline graphic (landscape): credentialing + cash-flow — month -8 start credentialing → month 0 open → month 3–4 first meaningful collections → month ~18 cash break-even. Alt text: "Urgent care credentialing and cash-flow timeline from pre-opening to break-even."
> 💡 ProTip: Build the loan draw so 6–12 months of operating expenses are funded at closing — SBA 7(a) allows it, and it's the difference between surviving the ramp and running dry.

## 9. FAQ Section

> 4 FAQs — included because first-time urgent care founders consistently ask these after reading a sample. Each is checked against the outline; none duplicate a main section.

**Q1:** How much does it cost to open an urgent care clinic?
**A1:** Summarize that a single-location clinic typically needs $0.8M–$1.5M in total funding; in this plan, $1.3M covers buildout, equipment, licensing, and a working-capital reserve. Point to the Startup Costs table.
**Source:** Experity cost guide / editorial judgment.
**Why not in main content:** It's a top-of-funnel question; the plan answers it in detail, but readers scan the FAQ first.

**Q2:** Why does an urgent care lose money in its first year even with steady patients?
**A2:** Explain the credentialing lag and the 60–90 day reimbursement delay — visits ramp before collections do, while fixed costs run in full. That's a working-capital issue, not a broken model.
**Source:** JUCM / the plan's Year 1 financials.
**Why not in main content:** The Payer Credentialing chapter explains the mechanism; this FAQ answers the blunt version new owners ask.

**Q3:** When should a new clinic start insurance credentialing?
**A3:** Six to eight months before opening, applying to the top 5–7 payers in parallel, because credentialing alone can take 180+ days and determines when billable revenue starts.
**Source:** JUCM / Experity.
**Why not in main content:** The chapter covers strategy; the FAQ gives the quick timing answer.

**Q4:** What do SBA lenders want to see in an urgent care loan application?
**A4:** A 15–25% owner injection, a DSCR around 1.25+, three-statement projections, a credentialing plan, and the standard document package (tax returns, financials, use of proceeds).
**Source:** Crestmont / SBA.
**Why not in main content:** The Funding Request chapter states this plan's specifics; the FAQ generalizes it for other readers.
