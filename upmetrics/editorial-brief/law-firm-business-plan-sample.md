## 2. Thesis & Key Messages

### Thesis Statement

> Harlow & Vance Law seeks $120,000 in startup financing to launch a lean, referral-driven family-law, estate-planning, and probate practice in the Raleigh metro — a firm that breaks even in Year 1 on conservative billable-hour and collection assumptions and funds its own growth from there.

### Key Messages

1. A solo/small law firm's revenue is not its billings — it is the smaller number left after realization and collection. A credible plan models all three layers.
2. Combining family law, estate planning, and probate is a deliberate business decision: the three practice areas smooth cash flow and feed each other referrals, rather than competing for the same hours.
3. Advance retainers sit in a trust account and are not the firm's operating cash until they are earned — a distinction that sinks firms that treat the trust balance as income.
4. Hiring a second attorney is a math decision, not an ego decision: it is justified only when the founder's billable capacity is maxed and the pipeline can cover a new salary within a defined window.
5. On modest, defensible numbers, a disciplined solo firm can pay its owner a real salary, service its debt, and still end Year 1 in the black.

## Why This Matters

- Most "law firm business plan" samples online are either empty how-to outlines or templates with copied-in, inconsistent numbers (one popular sample lists a $30,000 startup cost against a $34,350 itemized total). A genuinely worked, math-consistent example is rare and highly linkable.
- Solo and small firms rarely fail on legal skill — they fail on cash timing: slow collections, retainer confusion, and hiring too early. No competing sample explains this.
- Family law + estate planning + probate is one of the most common real-world solo-firm combinations, yet almost no sample plan models it specifically.
- "When can I afford to hire a second attorney?" is a top question for growing firms and is almost never answered with actual numbers — this page can own that answer.

## 8. Content Outline

> This outline follows a standard business-plan skeleton adapted to a small law firm. Chapter names, terminology, and emphasis use the firm's own vocabulary (matters, retainers, trust accounting, realization, billable capacity). Five sections are information-gain chapters the reader cannot find in a generic template — they are marked **[INFO-GAIN]** and are non-negotiable.

### Writing Tone

**For Sample Business Plan:**
- Write like a business consultant drafting a formal plan for a client. Third person throughout — "Harlow & Vance Law will..." never "we" or "I."
- Every claim backed by a specific number, date, or named detail from Sections 1B and 6B.
- Financial sections use the pre-calculated tables exactly — invent no new numbers.
- Replace vague language with specifics: "22% year-over-year revenue growth," not "significant growth."
- Use the firm's real name, the owner's name (Rachel Vance), and the location (Briarwood, NC) consistently.
- It must read as if it could be submitted to First Carolina Bank tomorrow.

**General rules:**
- Target reading level: Grade 8–9.
- Short paragraphs (3–4 sentences). Plain language. No passive voice. No AI buzzwords (see Appendix).

### Section Priority Labels

- 🔴 **MUST HAVE** — essential to the plan or the reader's core question.
- 🟡 **SHOULD HAVE** — important for depth.
- 🟢 **NICE TO HAVE** — adds differentiation.

#### H2: Why This Mix of Practice Areas Makes Business Sense
> ~500 words, 4–5 paragraphs | 🔴 MUST HAVE **[INFO-GAIN #1]**
> 🎯 Our angle: Treat the practice-area combination as a business decision, not a coincidence. Explain the cash-flow and referral logic no generic template covers.
> 🔗 Transition from previous: "The firm's three practice areas were not chosen at random — together they solve a cash-flow problem that sinks many new practices."
> 🚫 Avoid: Don't describe what family law / estate planning / probate are (that comes in Practice Areas). Stay on the business rationale.
> 📖 Reference: None — this is original analysis.

Explain the logic: (1) **Cash-flow smoothing** — family-law litigation is lumpy and hourly, estate-planning packages are predictable flat-fee cash collected up front, and probate is steady and often referral-driven; mixing them evens out the revenue line. (2) **Shared client lifecycle** — a divorce client often needs a new will; an estate-planning client's family later needs probate; the same household refers all three. (3) **One skill base, one back office** — the three areas share intake, document systems, and a courthouse relationship, so overhead is shared rather than tripled. (4) **Counter-cyclicality** — estate and probate work is relatively recession-resistant, hedging the discretionary swings in family law. Tie each point back to the firm's numbers where possible.

> 🖼️ Visual: Simple diagram showing the three practice areas feeding one another referrals (divorce → will → probate), with a note on how each contributes to monthly cash. Alt text: "How family law, estate planning, and probate refer clients to each other."
> 💡 ProTip: Pro Tip: A lender likes a diversified revenue mix for the same reason an investor does — no single practice area failing takes down the firm.

#### H2: How Billable Hours and Collections Determine Real Revenue
> ~650 words, 5–6 paragraphs | 🔴 MUST HAVE **[INFO-GAIN #2 + #3]**
> 🎯 Our angle: This is the chapter the reader can't get anywhere else. Show the three-layer cascade — hours worked → hours billed (realization) → cash collected (collection) — and explain why the trust/retainer balance is not operating cash.
> 🔗 Transition from previous: "Those fees are only potential revenue. What the firm actually banks depends on two numbers most new owners overlook."
> 🚫 Avoid: Don't get academic. Use the firm's own numbers to make it concrete. Don't conflate billings with revenue anywhere in the plan.
> 📖 Reference: None — original, benchmark-grounded analysis.

Walk through: (1) **Billable capacity** — a solo owner can't bill 2,000 hours while also running the firm; model the founder's realistic billable hours and why Year 1 productive capacity is limited. (2) **Realization** — not all worked hours get billed (write-downs, courtesy time); cite the ~88% industry realization benchmark. (3) **Collection** — not all billed work gets paid, and it arrives late; cite the ~90%+ collection rate and the 120–150 day industry collection lag, and tie it to the plan's 55-day AR assumption and the ~$40K Year 1 receivables balance. (4) **Retainers and trust accounting [INFO-GAIN #3]** — advance retainers go into the IOLTA trust account and belong to the client until fees are earned and invoiced; the firm cannot spend the trust balance, so a "full" trust account is not operating cash. This is why the plan raises a working-capital reserve. Connect directly to the negative Year 1 operating cash flow.

> 🖼️ Visual: **Cascade diagram / funnel** — "100 hours worked → ~88 billed → ~82 collected" style, with dollars. Alt text: "How worked hours become billed hours and then collected cash at a law firm."
> In the realization/collection paragraph, link ["build the revenue forecast"](https://upmetrics.co/blog/how-to-forecast-revenue); in the retainer/trust paragraph, link ["the difference between cash flow and profit"](https://upmetrics.co/blog/cash-flow-vs-profit).
> 💡 ProTip: Pro Tip: Treat the trust account as the client's money held in escrow. Move earned fees to the operating account only after you invoice against them — and never plan payroll around a trust balance.
