## 2. Thesis & Key Messages

### Thesis Statement

> Comal Street Kitchen is asking for a $100,000 SBA 7(a) loan to put one well-equipped taco truck on a fixed weekly route in Columbus. The plan holds up because every sales figure is a count a lender can check (services a week, orders per service, average order, bookings a month), Ohio's winter and lost days are built into the forecast, and the loan is covered 1.37 times in Year 1 (before one-time opening costs) and 2.69 times by Year 3 after paying the owner $52,000–$56,000 a year.

### Key Messages

1. **The forecast is a count of orders, not a market-share guess.** 280 planned services, 24 lost to weather and breakdowns, leaves 256 operated services. At 71.5 orders each and $14.00 per order, that's $256,200. Daniela's pop-ups averaged 96 orders in four hours, so the plan assumes less than she has already done.
2. **Catering is forecast on its own line.** 25 private bookings in Year 1 at an average of $2,600 (about 130 guests at $20 per guest) add $65,000. They're booked in advance, paid half up front, and they fill weekends that a street stop wouldn't.
3. **One set of ingredients keeps cost and waste down.** Every item is built from the same three proteins, rice, beans, salsas and toppings, so the truck carries less stock and throws less away. The menu blends to exactly a $14.00 average order and 32% food and packaging cost.
4. **Ohio's winter is in the numbers, not hidden in an annual average.** May–September brings in 62.0% of Year 1 sales, and January brings in 1.0%. Cash builds to $55,086 by November and falls to $31,956 by March. The lowest point is $18,548, in April, right after opening costs are paid.
5. **The owner, crew, site fees and repairs are paid before profit.** Daniela's $52,000 salary, 3,212 crew hours at $18.00 an hour, $10,248 of site and event fees, $12,600 of fuel and $7,200 of maintenance are all expenses before the loan payment.

## Why This Matters

- The current page tells readers how to write a food truck plan but never shows a finished one. Its sample tables don't reconcile, and it has no schedule, permits, commissary or owner pay. A complete, lender-ready example matches the other new-format samples in the library.
- Most published food truck samples either leave the financials blank or assume 30 operating days a month all year (one widely shared template does both). A plan that shows lost days, a winter slowdown and a separate catering forecast is more useful to a first-time owner and more believable to a bank.
- "Food truck business plan" readers are usually deciding whether to borrow. This plan answers the questions they actually have: how much to spend on the truck, how much to keep in reserve, how many orders a stop needs, and what the owner can pay herself.
- It shows Upmetrics' financial forecasting at work: a driver-based revenue forecast, monthly seasonality, a separate revenue stream, payroll, a full SBA loan schedule, debt coverage and a stress test.

### What to Borrow / What to Avoid

**Borrow:**
- Reference 1's lender-style section order and its Implementation Plan idea (personnel, timeline). Put the funding request, Year 1 sales target and seasonal effect up front.
- Reference 2's split between daily sales and event/catering revenue, its menu-pricing section with a target food cost, and its Ops & Management structure (equipment, daily workflow, compliance).
- Reference 3's Business Operations topic list (routine, schedule, commissary, equipment, permits, staffing), now answered with real details.

**Avoid:**
- Year-round 30-day months. This plan counts planned services, subtracts lost days, and shows each month separately.
- Empty or placeholder financial tables, and labor lumped together so the owner's pay disappears.
- Unsourced national market statistics. Use only the external links in Section 6, and label local figures as Daniela's own research.
- A menu that tries to do everything (Reference 1's pizza, burgers and tacos). One cuisine, shared ingredients, eight items.
