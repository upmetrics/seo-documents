## 2. Thesis & Key Messages

### Thesis Statement

> A six-court community tennis club in a growing suburb can repay a bank loan when its lease outlasts the loan, its memberships and programs fill evenings and weekend mornings first, and its numbers plan for the hours the Texas heat and rain take away. Baselark Tennis Club shows how an experienced pro in Frisco, Texas moves from a planned first-year loss to debt coverage of 2.43× in Year 2, with prices set beside free and $11 public courts.

### Key Messages

1. **Cheap courts aren't the gap; reliable ones are.** Frisco has more than 100 public courts at parks and schools, open first-come or at $11 for 90 minutes, but school courts open to the public only from 5:30 PM on weekdays, and coaching is not allowed at middle school courts. Baselark sells reserved courts, daytime access, coaching on site, and a club, not court access alone.
2. **The lease matters more than the rent.** Nadia turned down an offer at $4,200 a month on a three-year term with the courts as-is and the landlord keeping three courts in the busiest hours. It would have cost about $103,000 a year more than the lease she signed, once the court work, the shorter loan, and the lost court time are counted.
3. **Prime time is the real capacity.** Six courts are open 31,085 hours a year, but evenings and weekend mornings fill first: prime-time utilization rises from 62.0% to 77.8%. Growth after Year 2 comes from daytime members, morning clinics, and camps.
4. **Programs carry the club alongside dues.** Dues are about 42% of Year 2 revenue; lessons, clinics, the junior academy, and camps are about 49%, and they are how new families find the club.
5. **Conservative numbers, shown honestly.** Revenue $432,879 → $508,905 → $561,672; a −$35,492 pre-tax loss in Year 1 (including $32,200 of one-time costs), then $52,301 and $85,922. Break-even is $400,278 of revenue a year; debt coverage is 2.43× in Year 2, and a revenue shortfall above 10.4% would take it below 1.25×.

## Why This Matters

- **Tennis club plans online stop at the outline.** The sample plans and templates we found list chapter headings and revenue streams, but none shows court utilization, prime-time limits, or what a cheap lease does to a loan. Readers searching for a tennis club business plan want those answers with real numbers.
- **It answers a founder's real questions:** what six leased courts cost to open, what to charge next to free public courts, how memberships and coaching split the revenue, how many staff a small club needs, and how much cash it needs in the first slow months.
- **It shows lenders what they check first.** A lease that outlasts the loan, a Year 1 loss shown plainly, a break-even in court-hours, and a stress test read as more credible than a plan that assumes full courts every evening.

### What to Borrow / What to Avoid

**Borrow:**
- Reference 1's habit of measuring court use against peak time, its member-distance fact, and its dated action plan. Its four constraints (no secure agreement, dark courts, worn surfaces, no clubhouse) are exactly what Baselark's lease and startup budget solve.
- Reference 2's operating questions as a checklist: Baselark's Operations chapter answers each one (hours, access, peak rules, coverage by time of day).
- Reference 3's revenue-stream list and its point that outdoor clubs live with weather; Baselark puts numbers on both.

**Avoid:**
- Revenue streams without prices or volumes, and percentages without a source (Reference 3).
- Silence on the lease, resurfacing, and lighting (all three).
- Second-person advice inside the plan. The plan is written about Baselark.
