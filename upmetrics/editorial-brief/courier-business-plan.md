## 2. Thesis & Key Messages

### Thesis Statement

> Brisklane Courier is a two-van, owner-operated courier serving Boise-area businesses with scheduled contract routes and same-day deliveries, seeking an $85,000 SBA 7(a) loan. The plan rests on one idea: a courier earns money by filling van hours. Route stops booked every weekday fill the base hours at a low price per stop, on-demand jobs fill the gaps at a high price per job, and Brisklane grows by adding route customers and hired driving hours before it buys more vans.

### Key Messages

1. **Routes are the base, on-demand jobs are the margin.** Route stops make up 65% of Year 1 deliveries but only 38% of list revenue, at $14 a stop. They come back every weekday and are billed monthly. Same-day, rush, and supply runs earn $34–$65 each and fill the hours between route runs. Route stops grow to 69% of deliveries by Year 3, with route customers rising from 14 to 24.
2. **Two vans carry all three years.** Total driving hours rise from 2,764 to 4,335 (about 2.6 deliveries per driving hour). That puts the two vans at 54% / 70% / 85% of their 5,100 weekday hours. A third van comes only when route stops pass ~35 a weekday, so the forecast never pays for a vehicle before the work is there.
3. **The owner drives first, then sells.** Jamal Pruitt drives about 1,800 hours a year in Years 1–2. Hired driving grows from 964 hours to 2,935 hours by Year 3, and he moves ~400 hours into sales and dispatch. Drivers earn $21.50–$23.00 an hour, at or above the Boise metro median for light truck drivers ($21.34, May 2025).
4. **Net 30 invoices are the cash risk, and the plan budgets for them.** Business customers pay about 35 days after delivery, so receivables tie up $16,570 by the end of Year 1. A $30,400 working capital reserve covers the gap, and month-end cash never falls below $11,753 (July 2027).
5. **Honest, lender-ready numbers.** Year 1 is close to break-even at $487 pre-tax profit, after $11,400 of one-time launch costs, then $23,690 and $30,660. Debt coverage is 1.33× in Year 1 (1.98× before one-time costs), 2.59× in Year 2, and 2.93× in Year 3.

## Why This Matters

- **The current page has no sample in it.** Upmetrics' courier page is a 2023 "how to write" checklist with no business, no prices, and no financials. Readers searching "courier business plan" want a finished example they can follow, and that is what this rewrite gives them.
- **Courier demand keeps growing, but margins are thin.** U.S. couriers and local delivery services will bring in about $241.3 billion in 2026, up 4.0% in a year, while industry profit margins slipped from 9.3% in 2021 to 7.2% ([IBISWorld, July 2026](https://www.ibisworld.com/united-states/industry/couriers-local-delivery-services/1950/)). A sample that shows where a small courier's profit comes from is more useful than another market-size chart.
- **Published courier plans don't hold up.** One widely shown sample plans a 15-person company with nationwide and international service, a balance sheet that doesn't balance, and a break-even it can't compute. Another gives no prices or costs at all. None shows how many deliveries one van can carry or what Net 30 invoices do to cash.
- **Readers' real questions are about capacity and cash.** New courier owners want to know what to charge per stop, how many routes one van can run, when to hire the first driver, when a second or third van pays for itself, and how to survive waiting 30+ days to get paid. This plan answers each with one set of numbers.
- **Boise is a realistic mid-sized market.** It has a metro of about 845,000 people, the state capitol and courts, and two hospital systems. Ada County had 26 local courier businesses with 441 workers in 2025, so there is room for a small courier that serves the businesses the larger firms overlook.

**Q3:** How do courier companies price deliveries: per mile, per stop, or per hour?

Many small couriers combine flat zone prices for on-demand work with per-stop route pricing, as this sample does, and add a fuel surcharge clause. Advise working out a cost per stop or per hour first, then setting prices above it.
