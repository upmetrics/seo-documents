### Owner Bio

> **Jenna Castillo (38), founder, owner, and lead teacher,** has taught yoga in Chicago for 12 years. She holds the E-RYT 500 credential from Yoga Alliance, plus CPR and first aid certification. For the last five years she was lead teacher and studio manager at an independent studio in Lincoln Park (do not name it), where she built and adjusted a 60-class weekly timetable, hired and scheduled 14 teachers, and ran the studio's beginner program. She has lived in Lakeview for nine years and teaches about 10 classes a week at Stillfern in Year 1, mostly the early and midday classes. She invests $50,000 of personal savings and signs a personal guarantee on the SBA loan. She does not solicit her former studio's students; Stillfern's founding members come from the neighborhood pre-sale.

**The team (mention only in Operations):** 5 part-time instructors in Year 1, 6 in Year 2, and 7 in Year 3, each with at least RYT-200 training and current CPR certification, teaching 3–6 classes a week. Most have daytime jobs, which is why they teach early mornings, evenings, and weekends. They are W-2 employees, paid per class. A part-time studio coordinator runs the front desk at the busiest check-in times, answers member messages, and posts the weekly schedule and class clips on social media.

**Timeline anchors (use consistently):** Jenna starts planning in 2026 → LLC formed January 2027 → lease signed and SBA loan closes May 2027 → build-out June–August 2027 → founding-member pre-sale July–August 2027 (30 founding members) → opening Tuesday, September 7, 2027 → first January rush, January 2028 → monthly revenue first passes break-even, March 2028 → Year 2 begins September 2028 with 32 classes a week → Year 3 begins September 2029 with 36 classes a week and the first price increase → Year 3 ends August 2030 with 176 members and $191,593 in cash.

**Prices (Year 1; use in Classes & Pricing):**
- Intro offer: $49 for 3 weeks of unlimited in-person and online classes (first-time students only)
- Drop-in $26 · 5-class pack $115 (good for 2 months) · 10-class pack $210 (good for 4 months)
- Unlimited membership $159 a month (includes live online classes) · 8-class membership $125 a month · both on monthly autopay, with a one-month pause allowed each year
- Discounted membership rate for students, teachers, nurses, and first responders, and a lower rate for annual prepay (these average 8% off list across all members)
- Online membership $39 a month (unlimited live online classes) · online drop-in $15
- Private lessons: $110 single session; a 3-session pack lowers the average to about $105
- Year 3 increase: unlimited $165, 8-class $129, packs and drop-ins about 3% higher, private lessons $115 single

**Prices exclude Chicago's amusement tax.** Under the Chicago Municipal Code, membership dues to a club run on a membership basis are exempt, but per-class and per-admission fees are not. Stillfern adds the tax at checkout on drop-ins, class packs, and intro offers, and on online classes sold to Chicago residents, and passes it to the city. The tax is not Stillfern's revenue. (Details for the Operations chapter are in External Links, row 4.)

**Class styles (use in Classes & Pricing):** Vinyasa Flow (60 min), Slow Flow (60 min), Gentle & Restorative (60 min), Yin (60 min, evenings and Sunday afternoon), and Yoga Basics (75 min, for new students, Saturday and Wednesday). All classes are non-heated, and every class offers props and options for beginners.

## 2. Thesis & Key Messages

### Thesis Statement

> A neighborhood yoga studio makes money when its classes are full at the right times and its students become members who stay. Stillfern Yoga shows how one 24-mat room, a timetable built around Lakeview's work hours, membership-first pricing, and a careful weekly review of which classes fill can take a first independent studio from a planned first-year loss to steady profit, while paying the owner every year and repaying an SBA loan.

### Key Messages

1. **Memberships are the business.** Memberships rise from 50% of revenue in Year 1 to 60% in Year 3. In Chicago they are also exempt from the city's amusement tax, while drop-ins and packs are taxed, which is one more reason Stillfern prices to turn intro students into members.
2. **The timetable decides the money.** Weekday evenings and weekend mornings bring three-quarters of all visits. A class taught by a paid instructor needs about 6 students to cover the instructor and its share of the rent; a near-full evening class leaves about $253 after both.
3. **Stream what's already running.** Streaming an existing evening class costs $11.10 and leaves about $38.80; a separate online-only class loses money. Stillfern streams its evening classes and keeps online-only classes to a few early and Sunday slots.
4. **The first year is slow, and the plan says so.** 113 members by August 2028, a $44,643 pre-tax loss in Year 1 with Jenna still paid $48,000, and a $58,300 cash reserve that keeps month-end cash above $28,409.
5. **Steady growth, not a hockey stick.** Revenue grows 56.1% in Year 2 (after a ramp-up year) and 19.8% in Year 3; average attendance rises from 9.0 to 12.1 per class, about half the room.

## 8. Content Outline

> Built from the reference analysis and editorial judgment. ### Writing Tone

**For Sample Business Plan:**
- Write the way the business-plan-template-generator rules say: plain, warm, and written by an experienced studio owner. Use "we" or "Stillfern," whichever reads more naturally; the introduction speaks as "we" (Upmetrics), never "I."
- Active voice. Back every claim with a number, date, place, or named detail from Section 1B and Section 6B.
- Use the pre-calculated tables in Section 6B exactly as given. Do not invent new numbers.
- Use the trade's words, explained once where a lender might not know them: vinyasa, slow flow, yin, restorative, props, intro offer, class pack, unlimited membership, autopay, waitlist, drop-in, sub (substitute teacher), RYT-200, E-RYT 500, simulcast (a class taught in the room and streamed live at the same time), fill rate (the share of mats taken).
- Grade 8–9 reading level, one idea per sentence. Open each H2 plainly with one to three sentences that name Stillfern and the section's focus ("Stillfern Yoga sells memberships first, with class packs and drop-ins for students who aren't ready to commit."). No clever openers, no slogans, no yoga clichés ("find your flow," "breathe in, breathe out," "namaste").

## 9. FAQ Section

> 4 FAQs. None of these questions is answered in the plan chapters above.

**Q1:** How much does it cost to open a yoga studio?
**A1:** Say that this sample budgets $150,000 ($100,000 SBA 7(a) loan + $50,000 owner equity), with the build-out ($47,500) as the largest item and $58,300 kept as cash for the first year. Note that a teacher who rents space by the hour, or takes over a space that is already built out, can start for much less, and that the cost new owners miss is paying rent, teachers, and themselves while membership builds. Point readers to the guide on [opening a yoga studio](https://upmetrics.co/blog/how-to-start-yoga-studio-business).
**Source:** Section 6B + Reference 2 (startup ranges) + editorial judgment
**Why not in main content:** The plan states Stillfern's costs; this answers the general question readers bring from search.

**Q2:** How many members does a yoga studio need to break even?
**A2:** Explain that the answer depends on rent, teacher pay, and the owner's salary. In this sample, break-even is about $23,477 of revenue a month, or about 274 in-person visits a week, with the owner paid. Explain how to work it out: add up fixed monthly costs, then divide by what's left from each dollar after teacher pay and card fees (68% here). Advise building the estimate from members and visits per week, not from a guessed percentage growth rate.
**Source:** Section 6B (break-even) + editorial judgment
**Why not in main content:** The plan gives Stillfern's break-even; this explains the method for readers.

**Q3:** Should a new yoga studio offer online classes?
**A3:** Say that online classes work best as an extra for members, not a second business. Streaming a class that's already running costs little (in this sample, about $11 a class), while a separate online-only class can lose money unless enough people join it. Advise starting with a few streamed classes and only adding online-only classes at times members ask for.
**Source:** Section 6B (online class comparison) + editorial judgment
**Why not in main content:** The plan shows Stillfern's choice; this generalizes it for readers.

**Q4:** How do I adapt this sample plan for my own yoga studio?
**A4:** Tell readers to change five inputs first: their own prices and intro offer, the number of mats in their room, a first timetable they can staff, how many founding members they can sign before opening, and what they need to pay themselves. Then rebuild the financial tables from members, visits, and prices. Point out that the structure (revenue by line, a timetable with attendance by time slot, a slow first year with a cash reserve, and a monthly cash view) stays the same. **Source:** Editorial judgment
