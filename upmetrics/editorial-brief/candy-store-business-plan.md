## 2. Thesis & Key Messages

### Thesis Statement

> Wonderjar Sweets is asking for a $125,000 SBA 7(a) loan to open an affordable neighborhood candy store in Brookside, Kansas City. It does not try to beat the supermarket on a king-size chocolate bar. It sells the several hundred items the supermarket doesn't carry, in a store families want to visit, and it earns its money on about 80 transactions a day at an average of $15.50.

### Key Messages

1. **The plan runs on two numbers: transactions a day and the average purchase.** 80 transactions a day at $15.50 gives $446,400 in Year 1. Volume grows 10% a year at the same price. Break-even is about 65 transactions a day at steady-state costs.
2. **Wonderjar competes with the supermarket on range, not price.** Packaged candy sells near convenience-store prices. The money is in the 40-bin pick-and-mix wall (70% margin at shelf price), nostalgic candy, trending imports and gift boxes that grocery aisles don't stock.
3. **Buying and inventory are the owner's real skill.** Renee Okafor ran seasonal candy sets for a grocery chain for years. Wonderjar keeps about 60 days of stock, pre-books each holiday 4–6 weeks ahead, rotates the bins first-in, first-out, and holds shrink and freight to under 1% of sales.
4. **Four holidays drive the year, and the cash plan is built around them.** October, December, February and March (Easter) bring 43% of Year 1 sales. The slow months, January and September, lose a little cash. A $48,750 reserve keeps month-end cash above $61,592 all year.
5. **Honest, lender-ready numbers.** Year 1 shows a pre-tax profit of only $1,849 after $11,500 of one-time launch costs, then $23,544 and $35,347. Debt coverage is 1.21× in Year 1 (1.78× before one-time costs), 2.24× in Year 2, and 2.78× in Year 3.

## Why This Matters

- **Upmetrics has no candy store plan.** Searchers looking for a candy store example land on other sites. This page fills a gap in the Retail category.
- **Published candy store samples don't add up.** One widely shown sample lists startup costs that total about $24,000 more than its stated budget, and puts a year's rent above its Year 1 sales. Another gives no prices, margins or statements at all. A third shows a funding need of up to $844,000 with no explanation. None works from transactions a day and an average purchase.
- **Candy sales are at a record, but the volume isn't growing.** Americans spent a record $55 billion on chocolate, candy, gum and mints in 2025, yet unit sales fell for a fourth year as cocoa costs pushed prices up ([FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/03/10/nostalgia-new-flavors-drive-us-confectionery-growth/)). A plan has to show where its customers come from, not just point at the market size.
- **The seasonal cash problem is the one lenders ask about.** About 63% of U.S. confectionery sales come from Valentine's Day, Easter, Halloween and the winter holidays. A candy store plan needs a month-by-month cash view, and most samples skip it.
- **Kansas City is a market readers recognize.** The city had about 521,220 residents in July 2025, and Brookside is one of its best-known shopping districts. A plan set in a real neighborhood reads like one a lender would actually see.

### What to Borrow / What to Avoid

**Borrow:**

- Reference 2's "success factors" and "risk policy" ideas, turned into Wonderjar's own numbers.
- Reference 3's revenue range as a reality check: Wonderjar's $446K → $540K sits inside it.

**Avoid:**
- Budgets that don't add up. Every figure in our plan comes from Section 1B or Section 6B, and every table ties out.
- Revenue with no driver. Ours is transactions a day × average purchase, with a month-by-month Year 1 view.
- A big management team. Wonderjar is one owner, one shift lead, and part-time clerks.
- Global market figures. Use U.S. candy data and Kansas City data only.
- A luxury or candy-making concept. Wonderjar buys finished candy and prices for families.
- Second-person advice inside the plan chapters.
