## 2. Thesis & Key Messages

### Thesis Statement

> Stackline Freight seeks a $70,000 SBA 7(a) loan so that Keisha Lambert can put one non-CDL 26 ft liftgate truck to work on pallet freight for Ridgeport-area distributors. The plan shows that one truck, billed about 190–220 days a year, can pay her a salary of about $47,000–$54,000 and still cover the loan payment 2–3 times over.

### Key Messages

1. **The truck sets the business model.** A 25,995 lb GVWR truck with a liftgate means no CDL, no IRP and no IFTA, but it also caps payload at about 9,500 lb. Stackline sells to customers whose freight fits that box.
2. **Revenue is built from billable days, not hope.** 190 days in Year 1 (of ~250 available), $800 per day, with the gap reserved for ramp-up, repairs, maintenance and slow weeks.
3. **Direct customers first, brokers second.** Many brokers won't book a carrier whose authority is less than 30–90 days old, and broker spot rates for small trucks are thin. Stackline launches with letters of intent from two local shippers and uses load boards only to fill gaps.
4. **Every load has to pass a profit check.** Stackline's all-in cost is about $695 per billable day (fixed costs + loan + fuel + helper). A load that can't clear that, after counting empty return miles and dock wait time, gets countered or turned down.
5. **The plan pays the owner and the bank, and only then grows.** Owner salary sits in operating expenses. Debt service coverage is 1.99× in Year 1, and a second truck is added only after demand and cash reserves clear set triggers.

### What to Borrow / What to Avoid

**Borrow:**
- Reference 1's debt service coverage ratio. Lenders read it first, so we show it every year (1.99× / 2.76× / 3.27×).
- Reference 2's "contracts, not loads" framing, plus its note that brokers wait before booking new authority. That's why Stackline's launch customers are direct shippers.
- Reference 3's two-weight-line explanation (26,001 lb vs 10,001 lb). We turn it into a short compliance table and add the items it skips.

**Avoid:**
- Reference 1's mismatched numbers. Every figure in our executive summary must match Section 6B exactly.
- Reference 1's missing owner salary. A plan that pays the owner only from "what's left" is the most common mistake lenders flag.
- Reference 2's vagueness on economics. Every rate in our plan has a unit (hour, day, flat load) and a volume behind it.
- Long how-to explanations in general. This is a finished plan written in the third person, not a tutorial.
